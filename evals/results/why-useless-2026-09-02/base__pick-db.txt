Before sharding, exhaust simpler options — sharding adds massive operational complexity and is rarely the right first move.

**Do these first, in order:**

1. **Check your indexes.** Run `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` on your slow queries. Missing indexes are the #1 cause of slow reads at 40M rows. 40M rows with a good index is fast; without one, it's a full sequential scan.

2. **Check what you're actually selecting.** `SELECT *` on wide rows kills performance. Fetch only the columns you need.

3. **Look at connection overhead.** If you're opening many short-lived connections, add a connection pooler (PgBouncer). This is often misdiagnosed as a query problem.

4. **Add read replicas.** If reads are high-volume but write traffic is fine, a read replica routes SELECT load off the primary with near-zero schema changes.

5. **Partition the table.** If your queries filter by a range (date, region, ID range), declarative table partitioning in Postgres can give you most of sharding's benefits with far less complexity — it's a single-database operation.

6. **Consider a cache layer.** If the same rows are read repeatedly, Redis/Memcached in front of the DB eliminates the query entirely.

**Only consider sharding if:** you've exhausted the above and the bottleneck is write throughput or total data volume that won't fit on one machine (typically hundreds of GB to TB range).

Sharding means splitting data across multiple database servers. It forces your application to know which shard holds each row, complicates transactions, makes joins harder, and creates a long-term operational burden. Most teams that reach for it early regret it.

Start with `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on your slowest query — that single output will tell you more than anything else.
